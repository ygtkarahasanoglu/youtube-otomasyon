## 9. Insider notes (procurement / supply chain angle — analysis, not personal anecdote)
1. **Lot-splitting inflates the visible "project cost" story but also protects competition.** SCSNE is awarding the canal in dozens of separate lots (lock-by-lock, sector-by-sector: Montmacq lock ≈€96M, Oisy-le-Verger lock ≈€315M, Somme viaduct section >€440M, separate Oise-works lot ≈€60M), each independently tendered to French/Belgian heavy-civil groups (Bouygues, SPIE Batignolles, BESIX, NGE, Egis). This is standard practice for state-backed megaprojects to keep any single contractor from having outsized leverage — but it also means the public almost never sees one clean "bill of materials," only a drip of award notices, which is exactly why press coverage struggles to pin down a single trustworthy total cost (hence the 5.1bn→7.3bn→"maybe 10bn depending what you count" confusion documented above).
2. **Specialist lock-gate and hydraulic-steel capacity, not concrete, is usually the real bottleneck on canal megaprojects.** Earthworks and concrete are commodity-capacity items that dozens of European civil contractors can bid on; large mitre gates, hydraulic cylinders and the control/automation systems for 25-m-deep locks are a much smaller supplier pool (a handful of European hydraulic-steel fabricators). That concentration is a believable, generalizable reason heavy-civil megaprojects like this one slip years on a handful of structures (the locks, the viaduct) while the plainer 100+ km of earthworks proceeds roughly on schedule — consistent with what's actually happening here (locks and the Somme viaduct are the sectors still mid-construction while Sector 1 earthworks started back in 2022).
3. **EU co-financing locks in scope but also locks in delay risk.** Because roughly a third of the budget comes from EU Connecting Europe Facility tranches disbursed in multi-year packages (€176M 2007-13, €539M 2014-20, €276M 2021-26, plus top-up tranches), the project's cash flow is tied to Brussels' multi-annual budget cycles, not just French domestic budget decisions. That's a structural reason an infrastructure megaproject can be both "two-thirds funded and politically committed" and still face a real financing gap for its last mile — the same dynamic buyers see on a smaller scale when a supplier's own key sub-supplier runs on a different payment/budget cycle than the main contract.
